# Understanding High Net Worth Taxation and Optimization Strategies in France

This expert interview with tax lawyer Marc Bornozer provides a detailed overview of the fiscal environment facing high-net-worth individuals (HNWIs) in France. It examines the complex realities of tax optimization, the legal framework protecting taxpayers, the effectiveness of recent fiscal measures, and strategies for entrepreneurs and wealthy taxpayers to manage their finances amid political and economic uncertainties.

## 1. Tax Optimization Realities and the "French Tax Paradise" Paradox

Marc Bornozer highlights the sophisticated fiscal planning used by the ultra-rich to minimize their tax burdens within French law. Contrary to popular belief, France can be considered a **“tax haven” for the wealthy** due to legal protections and fiscal structuring tools:

- **HNWIs often encapsulate income in opaque holding structures** that enable them to **limit or even eliminate personal income and property taxes**.
- The **French tax code, notably Article 13 of the Declaration of Human Rights, protects taxpayers against confiscatory taxation**, capping tax rates around 70-75%.
- The **“Laffer Curve” principle applies:** excessive taxation ultimately reduces tax revenue as taxpayers optimize or relocate assets.
- Despite rising tax rates over recent years, **the elite manage tax exposure through planning, not by mass exodus**.
- France’s protections make it more favorable for HNWIs compared to countries like Switzerland or the UK, where tax authorities often tax latent income directly.
- The ability of the wealthy to **"live off their capital rather than income"** explains the paradox of low personal income taxes despite significant wealth.

## 2. New Fiscal Measures and Their Limited Impact

Recent fiscal initiatives, such as the **Zucman tax on holdings**, aim to target undeclared wealth but face constitutional hurdles:

| Measure           | Initial Rate/Scope              | Revised Rate/Scope                                    | Outcome                               |
|-------------------|-------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Zucman Tax        | 2% tax on total financial assets in holdings | Reduced to 0.2% on “luxury goods” in holdings (yachts, planes, gold, residences without proper rent) | Limited revenue expected (~1 million €), mainly symbolic |
| Holding Taxation   | Impact on holding structures  | Procedural delays and exemptions allow taxpayers time to exit scope | Minimal fiscal impact                |

- The Zucman tax is **unlikely to survive constitutional review at high rates** due to confiscatory concerns.
- The tax base has been restricted to a small set of luxury assets, **greatly limiting revenue potential**.
- Taxpayers have almost a year to reorganize, **further reducing the measure’s practical effect**.

## 3. Fiscal Planning: Ethical but Highly Technical

Bornozer emphasizes the difference between tax **fraud** and **legal tax planning (or fiscal “optimization”)**, favoring the term **“fiscal planning”**:

- Clients use **legitimate, legal methods to minimize tax**, applying their constitutional right to choose the least tax-burdened path.
- The line between **“aggressive tax planning”** and **fraud** is blurred legally and politically but remains distinct in enforcement.
- Morality debates are rejected in favor of **ethics based on legality and fairness**.

## 4. Comparing Tax Regimes: France Versus Other Jurisdictions

The interview compares France’s tax system with other countries, especially regarding residence and capital taxation:

- France’s **complex progressive tax system contrasts with simpler flat taxes in Eastern Europe (e.g., 10% in Romania) or the UK (two brackets)**.
- Countries like Switzerland or the UK tax latent income inside holdings or deem deemed notions to circumvent tax avoidance.
- Despite high nominal rates in France, **Article 13 safeguards prevent excessive taxation of latent income, creating effective relief for the wealthy**.
- Flat tax alternatives in France could simplify taxation but may face political and social resistance.

## 5. Residency Rules and Tax Controls

Becoming and remaining a non-resident for tax purposes in France involves strict compliance:

- Residency is assessed on multiple criteria including **center of economic interests, habitual abode, and family situation**.
- The **180-day rule is a rough guideline but not exclusive**; conflicts between countries are typically resolved by tax treaties.
- France’s tax authority has **anti-terrorism level resources** to detect false non-residency and can review taxpayer data up to 10 years retroactively.
- Controls target suspected non-compliance; their selection is investigative, not random.

## 6. Entrepreneurial Tax Strategies: From Sale to Reinvestment

The interview discusses the widely used **Article 150-0 B ter (Apport-cession) mechanism** which allows entrepreneurs to defer capital gains tax under conditions:

| Feature                          | Details                                                            |
|---------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Purpose                         | Defers taxation on capital gains from selling a business by reinvesting in qualifying professional assets within 3 years (minimum 70% reinvested) |
| Eligible Investments            | Commercial, industrial, agricultural, liberal professional activities, or control of such companies |
| Target Audience                 | Serial entrepreneurs planning to reinvest proceeds into new ventures |
| Risks                          | Reinvestment funds (e.g., labelled funds) can be risky; not recommended for retirees or non-entrepreneurs |
| Alternatives                   | Partial reinvestment, conservative assets like forests (low risk, ~1% yield), hotel investments, or para-hotel real estate |
| Benefits                      | Enables tax deferral, estate planning, and capital preservation for future generations |

This mechanism is often paired with the **Dutreil pact (transmission pact)** which reduces inheritance tax significantly and encourages keeping family businesses in France.

## 7. Real Estate Investment via Holding Structures and SCS Companies

Bornozer explains how **Société en Commandite Simple (SCS)** is a more efficient legal form than the common Société Civile Immobilière (SCI) for real estate investments:

- SCS has two types of partners:
  - **Commanditaires** (limited liability; like silent investors).
  - **Commandités** (unlimited liability; actively manage the company).
- Tax Effects:
  - Income to commanditaires is subject to corporate income tax.
  - Income to commandités is taxed as personal income.
- This dual regime allows investors to **deduct depreciation and expenses at the corporate level**, reducing taxable income.
- At resale, the **taxable gain for commandités is treated as real estate capital gains with favorable abatements**, lowering the overall tax burden.
- Recommended for **investments over €750,000** due to complexity and costs.
- Explains the high overall effective tax rates on rental income (up to ~62%) including income tax, social charges (17.2%), and inheritance taxes.

## 8. Holdings, Distribution, and the Flat Tax Challenge

The tax advantage of using a holding company lies in controlling when to extract income:

- Capital accumulates tax-deferred **until distributed**—distribution triggers the flat tax (PFU) currently ~30%, but can reach **up to 38.6% including exceptional contributions** for high incomes.
- Buying a personal residence via a holding is discouraged due to:
  - Double taxation on distributions.
  - Loss of capital gains exemption on the primary residence.
  - Potential application of the 20% holding tax.
- Holding companies ideally invest in financial instruments and operational businesses to leverage tax deferral.

## 9. Asset Protection and International Structuring Risks

Regarding relocation of holdings or assets abroad, Bornozer warns:

- Moving a holding’s registered office out of France (e.g., Luxembourg) is risky because French tax authorities may reclassify the entity as French due to shareholder/management presence (“requalification”).
- Holding assets abroad (custody) is better for **protection against banking crises or political risk**, not aimed at tax evasion.
- The recent **Sapin 2 law limiting insurance contract redemptions** raises liquidity risks.
- Insurance contracts represent a creditor relationship with the insurer, protected only up to €100,000 under EU schemes.
- Historical precedents of state levies on bank accounts or forced loans are possible but unlikely in current contexts; precautionary diversification remains prudent.

## 10. Interaction with Policymakers and Future Outlook

Bornozer notes increased dialogue between tax experts and French lawmakers, especially after government changes reducing unilateral law-making power:

- The **Institute of Tax Lawyers actively lobbies for clear, stable, and predictable tax rules**.
- Some proposals include safeguarding the Dutreil pact and avoiding broad taxation of company cash reserves.
- Future tax hikes are expected more likely in inheritances rather than holdings.
- A flat tax reform could simplify the system but risks losing client advisory work for lawyers.
