# How to Properly Value Companies Beyond the PE Ratio

This video provides a detailed framework for valuing publicly traded companies by categorizing them into four distinct types based on what the investor is actually buying: **assets, equity (funds), promises, or earnings**. It critiques the common overreliance on the **Price-to-Earnings (PE) ratio** and offers tailored methods to value companies depending on their sector and business model. The presenter emphasizes the importance of contextualizing valuation metrics to avoid major mistakes and optimize investment decisions.

## 1. The Problem with the PE Ratio

The PE ratio is often seen as a universal valuation tool, but this is a **fundamental misconception**. The PE is:

- **Misused in 99% of cases** by investors who apply it to inappropriate companies.
- Particularly misleading for companies that do not fit into the "earnings" category (e.g., asset-heavy firms or high-growth startups).
- Resulting in wrong conclusions such as buying expensive companies on low PE multiples during peak profit cycles or avoiding companies with high or negative PEs during downturns.

**Key insight:** PE ratio should be used cautiously and only for companies where earnings are stable and a proper reflection of cash generation.

## 2. The Four Categories of What You Are Buying

Before valuing a company, the essential question is: **"What am I actually buying?"** There are four main categories:

| Category        | Typical Companies                              | Valuation Focus                                | Valuation Method Example                              |
|-----------------|-----------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| **Assets**      | Mining, oil & gas, real estate, asset-heavy   | Physical assets minus debt                     | Net Asset Value (NAV) / Discounted Asset Value       |
| **Equity / Funds** | Banks, insurers, asset managers               | Equity book value including debt as operational capital | Tangible Book Value per Share                          |
| **Promises**    | High-growth startups, biotech firms            | Future revenue or future earnings promises     | Revenue multiples (e.g., Price to Sales, ARR multiples) |
| **Earnings**    | Mature, profitable companies with predictable cash flows | Normalized earnings or free cash flow          | PE ratio or Price to Free Cash Flow                    |

### 2.1 Assets Valuation

Asset-based companies derive value from their tangible holdings:

- Example: The presenter uses **Parex**, an oil company, as a case study.
- Valuation method involves estimating proven reserves (with ~90% extraction probability), valuing them against scenarios of future commodity prices (e.g., $55-$90 per barrel).
- These future asset values are discounted (DCF) by a risk-adjusted rate incorporating country and company risks.
- Divide total discounted value by shares outstanding to get intrinsic stock price.
- Result: Parex was fundamentally undervalued when bought at $12 per share against an intrinsic value of $30.

**Important:** Using PE here is misleading, since profits fluctuate widely due to commodity cycles, leading to distorted PE multiples (very low at the top and very high or negative at the bottom).

### 2.2 Equity / Funds Valuation

For banks, insurers, or asset managers:

- The company uses **debt as an operational tool or “raw material”** to generate profits, so debt is not subtracted from assets.
- Valuation should focus on **tangible book value per share** or **equity value**, not enterprise value to EBITDA.
- Using EBITDA multiples or PE ratios incorrectly can cause major errors.

### 2.3 Promises Valuation

High-growth companies without profits yet—e.g., SaaS startups, biotech firms:

- Investors are buying potential future revenues or earnings.
- Valuation is based on **top-line metrics** such as **Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR)** or sales multiples.
- PE ratio is inappropriate here due to negative or zero earnings.
- For biotech, valuation considers speculative product pipelines rather than current earnings.

### 2.4 Earnings Valuation

For mature companies with predictable cash flows (e.g., Coca-Cola, Google):

- PE ratio is more appropriate but still must be normalized.
- Adjustments include accounting for:
  - Stock-based compensation distortions.
  - Non-cash items such as impairments or revaluations.
  - Capital expenditures (CapEx) effects.
- The presenter argues **Price to Free Cash Flow** is often a better multiple than PE for normalizing profitability.

## 3. The Role of Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Across Types

DCF is a unifying concept behind all valuation methods but applied to different fundamentals:

| Category   | Basis for DCF                                  |
|------------|------------------------------------------------|
| Assets     | Cash flows from extracted or sold assets' value |
| Equity     | Future returns on equity including cost of capital |
| Promises   | Projected future revenue or earnings streams     |
| Earnings   | Expected free cash flows adjusted for risks      |

The discount rate incorporates company-specific, sector, and country risk parameters.

## 4. Portfolio Diversification and Valuation Strategy

- The presenter warns **against only holding tech or earnings-driven companies**.
- Investing across different categories—assets, earnings, equity, and promises—can reduce portfolio risk and better capitalize on diverse market opportunities.
- Promises are risky and often overvalued; instead, investing in small caps with **hidden growth potential (upsell projects)** is a more attractive option.
- Always identify what you are paying for before applying valuation multiples.

## 5. Examples and Important Considerations

- Google’s recent earnings were boosted by a $33 billion positive non-cash revaluation linked to its investment in Anthropic, inflating net income by 50% on paper, but without corresponding cash flow.
- This highlights the necessity of focusing on **free cash flow over net income** when using PE multiples.
- The presenter strongly emphasizes understanding **different approaches for each company type** to avoid common valuation pitfalls.

## Summary of Valuation Methods by Company Type

| What You Buy | Suitable Valuation Metric(s)                 | Common Mistakes if Using PE         |
|--------------|---------------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| Assets       | Net Asset Value (NAV), Discounted Asset Value | PE misleading due to profit cyclicality |
| Equity / Funds | Tangible Book Value per Share, Equity Value | Using EV/EBITDA or PE causes errors |
| Promises     | Revenue multiples, ARR multiples             | PE irrelevant with no profits      |
| Earnings     | PE (normalized), Price to Free Cash Flow     | Must normalize earnings, adjust for non-cash items |

## Key Insights

- **PE ratio is not inherently bad but widely misapplied.**
- Always start valuation by asking: **"What am I buying?"**
- Use the valuation method that aligns with the nature of the company’s value driver.
- Discounted cash flow methods are fundamental but adapt the base cash flows or assets accordingly.
- Normalize earnings and emphasize free cash flow for mature companies.
- Diversify portfolio by mixing asset-heavy, earning-based, and growth-oriented companies.
- Beware of accounting distortions inflating reported earnings without underlying cash flow support.

This framework empowers investors to conduct thoughtful, sector-appropriate valuations that avoid common traps and improve investment decision-making.
